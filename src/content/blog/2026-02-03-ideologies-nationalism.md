## Nationalism

Nationalism is the belief that a shared national identity—rooted in culture, language, history, or ethnicity—should be the primary basis for political organization and loyalty. At its best, nationalism fosters unity, collective purpose, and self-determination. At its worst, it narrows belonging, elevates exclusion, and turns identity into a weapon.

Unlike liberalism or socialism, nationalism is not inherently economic. It is emotional, symbolic, and deeply psychological—concerned with *who “we” are* before what policies we support.

---

## Core Ideas

- **Nation before individual**
  The interests of the nation are prioritized over personal or global concerns.

- **Shared identity**
  A common culture, language, religion, or historical narrative binds people together.

- **Sovereignty and self-determination**
  Nations should govern themselves without external interference.

- **Collective loyalty**
  Civic duty and national pride are seen as moral virtues.

---

## Why Nationalism Appeals

Nationalism often gains strength during periods of uncertainty—economic instability, cultural change, or perceived loss of status. It offers clarity and belonging when systems feel distant or abstract.

- Creates a strong sense of unity
- Mobilizes people toward common goals
- Resists foreign domination or imperialism
- Reinforces cultural preservation

For colonized or oppressed populations, nationalism has historically been a powerful force for liberation.

---

## Where It Becomes Dangerous

The same forces that unite can also divide.

- **Exclusion and “othering”**
  Defining who belongs often implies who does not.

- **Suppression of dissent**
  Criticism may be framed as betrayal.

- **Ethnic or religious nationalism**
  Can marginalize minorities within the state.

- **Expansionism and conflict**
  National pride can slide into aggression toward others.

When national identity becomes rigid or sacred, it can override democratic norms and human rights.

---

## Nationalism in Practice

Nationalism rarely appears alone. It often merges with other ideologies:

- **Civic nationalism** → aligned with democracy and equal citizenship
- **Ethnic nationalism** → tied to ancestry, bloodline, or religion
- **Economic nationalism** → protectionism and domestic industry
- **Authoritarian nationalism** → centralized power justified by national survival

The outcomes depend less on nationalism itself and more on how narrowly identity is defined—and who controls that definition.

---

## The Tension at Its Core

Nationalism asks a difficult question:

> *Can a shared identity unite without excluding?*

In a globalized world, nationalism continues to shape politics by pulling against internationalism, multiculturalism, and global cooperation. Its power lies in emotion, memory, and meaning—forces that reason alone rarely overcomes.
